HVAC BIM & engineering

The engineering position

Duct depth is the constraint the rest of the project pays for

Rectangular ductwork is the largest single object in most ceiling voids, it cannot be rerouted as cheaply as cable tray, and it is usually drawn last. The result is a familiar sequence: the void is set architecturally, the mechanical route does not fit, and the resolution is either a bulkhead nobody wanted or an aspect-ratio change nobody checked the consequence of. We resolve the primary mechanical route first, and treat every subsequent change to it as an engineering change rather than a drafting one.

Aspect ratio is an engineering decision, not a coordination workaround

Flattening a duct to clear a beam changes its hydraulic diameter, its pressure drop and its fan duty. Done once it is invisible; done eleven times along a run it is a plant selection problem discovered at commissioning. We report those changes to the responsible engineer as they are made, which is the only point at which they are cheap to argue about.

Plant access is designed, not left over

Coil pull space, filter access, damper access and tube pull are dimensioned requirements. They are also the requirements most often satisfied by whatever space happens to remain. We model them as clearance zones and test them as rules, so an access failure is a finding rather than an argument on site.

How it runs

The sequence, and why it is in this order

Sequence is not a formality on this kind of work. Most of the expensive rework we are asked to fix was produced by doing step four before step two.

01

Design basis

Confirm the HVAC design, equipment selections and criteria — supplied by your engineer, or calculated by us where that is explicitly in scope.

02

Zoning

Allocate the mechanical zone in the ceiling void and the riser allocation, and agree it with the other disciplines in writing.

03

Primary distribution

Route the largest ducts and mains first, against structure. Everything that follows is fitted to this, not the other way round.

04

Terminals and equipment

Place terminals, equipment and accessories, with tags and schedules populated from the model.

05

Access and clearance

Test coil pull, filter access, damper access and valve access as clearance rules across the whole model.

06

Documentation

Drawings, schedules and shop drawings issued with the QA rule set run.

What we need to start

The inputs that decide whether a start is a real start

Missing any of these is survivable. Not knowing which of them are missing is not, so we establish it at enquiry stage rather than at forty per cent.

  • Architectural model or plans with ceiling heights and compartment lines

  • Selected plant data, if already procured

Scope boundary

What this appointment does not include

Published because a scope boundary discovered at month three is a dispute, and the same boundary stated at enquiry stage is just information.

  • Load calculations, duct and pipe sizing and specialist smoke-control analysis are not included unless design responsibility, inputs and qualified engineering resources are agreed.
  • We do not perform CFD analysis, on-site commissioning or balancing.
  • We do not certify compliance with a jurisdiction's mechanical code; your engineer of record certifies.

FAQ

Questions we are asked at enquiry stage

Which HVAC systems do you model?

Ducted air systems, ventilation and exhaust, car-park ventilation, smoke-extract ductwork, chilled and condenser water piping, VRF/VRV refrigerant piping, split and ducted units, and mechanical plant rooms.

Do you calculate loads, or only model what we give you?

By default we model to the HVAC design your engineer supplies, and we will tell you if the modelled network disagrees with it. Load calculations and sizing are offered only where they are explicitly in scope and supported by qualified engineers.

Can you produce HVAC shop drawings?

Yes — installation layouts and shop drawings derived from the coordinated model. State the level of detail at enquiry stage, because it changes how the model is authored.

How do you handle a ceiling void that is too shallow?

We report it as a finding with the options and their consequences — aspect ratio change, a bulkhead, a re-zoning, or a raised slab-to-slab. What we will not do is quietly flatten the duct until it fits.
